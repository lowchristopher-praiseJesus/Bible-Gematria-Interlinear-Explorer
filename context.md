### Matthew
**1. Historical Setting:** Not explicitly detailed in the provided text. 
**2. Cultural Background:** The culture anticipated the coming Messiah through over 300 Old Testament prophecies. Tax collectors were highly despised as Jews working for the Roman government, often skimming money. The genealogy challenges cultural norms by highlighting that God uses flawed people, like prostitutes and deceivers, to accomplish His divine plan.
**3. Author and Audience:** Written by Matthew (also known as Levi), a former tax collector and one of Jesus' twelve disciples, primarily to a Jewish audience. 
**4. Literary Context:** It is the first book of the New Testament, following the Old Testament book of Malachi, and provides the legal genealogy of Jesus through Joseph back to King David and Abraham. 
**5. Genre and Style:** A Gospel presenting Jesus as the King of the Jews, quoting over 100 Old Testament verses to convince its Jewish audience. Early church fathers associated it with the symbol of a lion.
**6. Language and Translation:** Utilizes a Jewish numerical system called gematria, structuring the genealogy into three sets of 14 generations (the numerical value for "David"). Refers to Jesus as "the Christ," the Greek transliteration of Messiah.
**7. Theological Themes:** Supremacy of Christ, the Kingdom of Heaven (used 32 times), and Grace for the Imperfect.
**8. Immediate Purpose:** To verify to Jewish readers that Jesus is the Christ by showing He fulfilled Messianic prophecies, and to demonstrate that in Christ, people receive a new identity and a new start.

### Mark
**1. Historical Setting:** Written around 63 AD while Paul awaited trial before Emperor Nero. Much of the narrative occurs in Capernaum, a strategic village on the major Via Maris trade route.
**2. Cultural Background:** Tax collectors were viewed suspiciously, and professions like fishing were common. The culture was marked by an awareness of the supernatural, demonic possession, and the need for physical healing, with religious life centered around the synagogue.
**3. Author and Audience:** Written by Mark (John Mark), who relied heavily on the eyewitness testimony of the Apostle Peter. It was written primarily for a Roman/Gentile audience.
**4. Literary Context:** Not explicitly detailed in the provided text.
**5. Genre and Style:** Not explicitly detailed in the provided text.
**6. Language and Translation:** Originally written in Greek. Mark frequently translates or explains Hebrew/Jewish concepts and terms because he is writing to Romans.
**7. Theological Themes:** Jesus as the lowly servant who came to minister, the power of prayer, and Jesus' authority over evil and demonic forces.
**8. Immediate Purpose:** To proclaim Jesus' purpose to preach in the next towns and to encourage believers to develop a discipline of prayer ("pray like Jesus").

### Luke
**1. Historical Setting:** The opening events mark the end of 400 "silent years" during which God had not spoken through prophets, taking place under Roman government influence [Conversation History].
**2. Cultural Background:** The religious culture maintained a priesthood of about 20,000 where duties were chosen by lottery. Socially, barrenness was a significant hardship and legal grounds for divorce. Luke's writing reflects a more inclusive cultural outlook, emphasizing that Jesus came for all people, including Gentiles.
**3. Author and Audience:** Written by Luke, a Gentile physician and companion of Paul, specifically to a man named Theophilus, but intended for a broader Gentile audience.
**4. Literary Context:** The first volume of a two-part work (Luke-Acts) containing unique material not found in other Gospels, based on serious investigation of eyewitness accounts.
**5. Genre and Style:** A Gospel emphasizing the humanity of Jesus as the "Son of Man," incorporating specific medical details due to Luke's profession.
**6. Language and Translation:** Not explicitly detailed in the provided text.
**7. Theological Themes:** The humanity of Christ, universal salvation for everyone, God's faithfulness to His promises, and the power of the Holy Spirit.
**8. Immediate Purpose:** To provide certainty to believers about what they had been taught, record an orderly historical account, and demonstrate the fulfillment of prophecy.

### John
**1. Historical Setting:** The last Gospel written, around 85–90 AD, by the last surviving apostolic eyewitness.
**2. Cultural Background:** Engages heavily with Greek philosophy, specifically the concept of *Logos* (Divine Reason). It served as a defense against Arianism and bridged Jewish and Gentile cultures.
**3. Author and Audience:** Written by the Apostle John, an intimate eyewitness, for both believers and seekers.
**4. Literary Context:** Mentions only eight miracles (six unique to this book) and traces Jesus' origins back further than the Synoptic Gospels—to the beginning before time.
**5. Genre and Style:** A Gospel symbolized by the eagle, structured to immediately develop the premise of Jesus' deity.
**6. Language and Translation:** Uses the Greek word "Logos" (Word), the imperfect tense to imply continuous eternal action, and "skinu" to describe Jesus "pitching a tent" or tabernacling among humanity.
**7. Theological Themes:** The absolute deity of Christ, the Trinity, Jesus as the Creator, and the contrast between Light and Darkness.
**8. Immediate Purpose:** To inspire belief that Jesus is the Son of God, to show Jesus as God's revelation personified, and to explain the Great Exchange of His sinless life for our sinful ones.

### Acts
**1. Historical Setting:** Covers roughly 30 years from ~30 AD to 62 AD, tracing the church's expansion under the Roman Empire from Jerusalem to Rome.
**2. Cultural Background:** Captures the transition from Old Testament Judaism to the New Covenant, moving from a Jewish center to pagan Greco-Roman cities. Early Christians faced significant persecution and social risk.
**3. Author and Audience:** Written by Luke to Theophilus and the broader church.
**4. Literary Context:** Serves as the vital historical bridge between the Gospels and Paul's Epistles, ending abruptly to suggest the work is ongoing.
**5. Genre and Style:** A fast-paced, action-oriented historical narrative detailing the "Acts of the Holy Spirit".
**6. Language and Translation:** Written in Greek, utilizing key terms like *Dunamis* (dynamic power) and *Martus* (witness/martyr).
**7. Theological Themes:** The central role of the Holy Spirit, the Great Commission, the formation of the Church, and the spiritual Kingdom of God.
**8. Immediate Purpose:** To show the Spirit's empowerment available to believers, provide a blueprint for church expansion, and validate the Christian movement as divine rather than a political insurrection.

### Romans
**1. Historical Setting:** Written ~57–58 AD from Corinth, about three years before Paul arrived in Rome as a prisoner awaiting trial before Nero.
**2. Cultural Background:** The Roman church was a blend of Jewish (law-based) and Gentile (polytheistic) believers, creating cultural challenges.
**3. Author and Audience:** Written by the Apostle Paul to the believers in Rome, a church he did not plant and had never visited.
**4. Literary Context:** Follows Acts in biblical placement but not chronologically, uniquely focusing on God's redemption plan rather than specific church problems.
**5. Genre and Style:** An Epistle structured to first present the "bad news" of human sinfulness across three categories of people to establish the need for the Gospel.
**6. Language and Translation:** Greek terms include *Euangelion* (Gospel), *Charus* (Grace), *Pistis* (Faith), *Orge* (Wrath), and *Hagios* (Saint).
**7. Theological Themes:** The Gospel, Grace over human effort, Faith, God's Wrath, and Creation as a witness.
**8. Immediate Purpose:** To explain God's plan of redemption, establish universal accountability for sin, and present salvation as the remedy.

### 1 Corinthians
**1. Historical Setting:** Written around 55 AD from Ephesus to Corinth, a bustling, wealthy seaport known for extreme corruption.
**2. Cultural Background:** The city was so corrupt a new verb was coined ("to act like a Corinthian"). The pervasive influence of the Temple of Aphrodite and diverse social classes created massive friction within the congregation.
**3. Author and Audience:** The Apostle Paul wrote to the church in Corinth [Implicit in 50].
**4. Literary Context:** Not explicitly detailed in the provided text.
**5. Genre and Style:** An Epistle with a very direct, confrontational, and pastoral tone, pulling no punches.
**6. Language and Translation:** Uses *hagios* (sanctified/set apart) to remind them of their identity, and *koinonia* to describe fellowship as a relationship with Jesus.
**7. Theological Themes:** Spiritual immaturity vs. maturity, unity vs. division (condemning hero worship), the power of the cross, and sanctification.
**8. Immediate Purpose:** To correct behavioral infighting, define true spiritual maturity, and address worldliness infiltrating the church.

### 2 Corinthians
**1. Historical Setting:** Written ~57–58 AD from Macedonia, roughly a year after 1 Corinthians, to the capital of Achaia.
**2. Cultural Background:** Corinth had 700,000 people, 500,000 of whom were slaves. Its status as a major port brought extreme wealth and deep social challenges.
**3. Author and Audience:** Written by Paul to the Corinthian church; it is the most autobiographical and personal of all his letters.
**4. Literary Context:** Follows 1 Corinthians, revealing Paul not just as an apostle, but as a man experiencing profound heartache and exhaustion.
**5. Genre and Style:** An Epistle with a vulnerable and comforting tone, using the words "comfort" or "consolation" ten times in the first chapter alone.
**6. Language and Translation:** Uses *Parakaleo* (to call alongside/comfort) and refers to God as the "Father of mercies".
**7. Theological Themes:** The God of all comfort, suffering with a purpose to comfort others, finding strength in weakness, and the roles of the Trinity.
**8. Immediate Purpose:** Not explicitly detailed in the provided text.

### Galatians
**1. Historical Setting:** Written ~49 AD, potentially Paul's very first epistle, directed to a province/region in Asia Minor (Turkey).
**2. Cultural Background:** Judaizers were heavily influencing the culture, pushing legalism and requiring Gentiles to follow Jewish customs (like circumcision) for salvation.
**3. Author and Audience:** Written by Paul to the "churches of Galatia" with a highly agitated, stern tone, skipping his usual complimentary opening.
**4. Literary Context:** Establishes the simplicity of the Gospel early on, detailing the human need for rescue.
**5. Genre and Style:** A circular Epistle of rebuke and correction, featuring an urgent defense of Paul's apostolic authority.
**6. Language and Translation:** Central words include *Charis* (Grace) and *Anathema* (Accursed/eternally condemned) to emphasize the single true Gospel.
**7. Theological Themes:** Grace alone by faith alone, liberty in Christ from legalism, and the sufficiency of Christ ("Jesus plus anything ruins everything").
**8. Immediate Purpose:** To combat legalistic false teachings, protect the purity of the Gospel, and reclaim the believers' identity in Christ.

### Ephesians
**1. Historical Setting:** Written ~60–62 AD while Paul was a prisoner in Rome; addressed to Ephesus, a wealthy and influential Roman hub in Asia Minor.
**2. Cultural Background:** Not explicitly detailed in the provided text.
**3. Author and Audience:** Written by Paul to the church in Ephesus, a congregation he spent three years with.
**4. Literary Context:** One of the four Prison Epistles, structurally divided into doctrine/position (chapters 1-3) and practice/conduct (chapters 4-6).
**5. Genre and Style:** An encouraging apostolic Epistle meant to strengthen believers' understanding rather than correct a specific heresy.
**6. Language and Translation:** Repeatedly uses "In Christ" or "In Him" to define identity, and describes God's grace as being "lavished" in abundant supply.
**7. Theological Themes:** Identity in Christ, the Sovereignty of God in election, the mystery of the united Church, and spiritual warfare (Armor of God).
**8. Immediate Purpose:** To answer identity confusion, affirm the church's cultural significance, and provide stability amid chaos.

### Philippians
**1. Historical Setting:** Written in 62 AD from prison in Rome to a church in Macedonia (Greece) that Paul had planted 10 years earlier.
**2. Cultural Background:** An exclusively Gentile city of ~10,000–15,000 people; Paul utilizes cultural metaphors of an Olympic race to emphasize perseverance.
**3. Author and Audience:** Written by Paul to the Philippian church, expressing a deeply warm, loving, and affectionate relationship without the need for heavy correction.
**4. Literary Context:** A Prison Epistle focusing early on Paul's confidence that God will finish the work He started.
**5. Genre and Style:** An Epistle characterized by a joyful, triumphant, and encouraging tone despite Paul's imprisonment.
**6. Language and Translation:** The word "Joy" (or "rejoice") is used 14 times, utilizing language that expresses confident realization.
**7. Theological Themes:** Joy, God's Faithfulness, Perseverance in the spiritual race, and the Sovereignty of Christ.
**8. Immediate Purpose:** To encourage believers not to give up, express abiding joy, and assure them of God's continuing work in their lives.

### Colossians
**1. Historical Setting:** Written between 60–61 AD while Paul was imprisoned in Rome awaiting trial before Nero, addressed to a city in modern-day Turkey.
**2. Cultural Background:** Influenced by a "distorted" mix of Greek philosophy, Jewish legalism, and Eastern mysticism, including the worship of angels.
**3. Author and Audience:** Paul and Timothy wrote to the church at Colasse, which Paul had never actually visited (founded by Epaphras).
**4. Literary Context:** A Prison Epistle that leads directly into a robust defense of the supremacy of Christ.
**5. Genre and Style:** An Epistle utilizing contrasting metaphors between the "Kingdom of Light" and "Kingdom of Darkness".
**6. Language and Translation:** Uses literal concepts (like physical light) to communicate deep spiritual and metaphorical truths.
**7. Theological Themes:** The Two Kingdoms, Christology (supremacy of Jesus), and Redemption through the forgiveness of sins.
**8. Immediate Purpose:** To combat an unknown heresy promoting "secret knowledge" and remind believers of their completeness in Christ.

### 1 Thessalonians
**1. Historical Setting:** An early letter written ~51 AD from Corinth; Paul had only stayed in Thessalonica for three weeks before a mob chased him out.
**2. Cultural Background:** A largely Gentile/pagan city hostile to Christians, who were accused of acting contrary to Caesar by claiming Jesus as king.
**3. Author and Audience:** Not explicitly detailed in the provided excerpt (though historically written by Paul to the Thessalonian believers).
**4. Literary Context:** Not explicitly detailed in the provided excerpt.
**5. Genre and Style:** An Epistle with an affectionate, urgent tone utilizing "family language," notably referencing the Second Coming at the end of every chapter.
**6. Language and Translation:** Uses *Ekklesia* to distinguish the church from political gatherings, and a word for "wait" that implies expectant readiness.
**7. Theological Themes:** The imminent Second Coming, Faith/Love/Hope, divine Election, and Sanctification.
**8. Immediate Purpose:** To encourage suffering believers, clarify doctrine regarding Christians who die before Christ returns, and call for readiness.

### 2 Thessalonians
**1. Historical Setting:** Written ~51–52 AD from Corinth, merely a few months after the first letter, as believers lived under Roman persecution.
**2. Cultural Background:** The young church faced specific physical suffering in a pagan city and cultural misinformation/forgeries about the end of the world.
**3. Author and Audience:** Written by Paul, Silas, and Timothy to the Thessalonian church, with Paul acting as a proud spiritual mentor.
**4. Literary Context:** A short, three-chapter follow-up letter diving into technical prophetic details regarding the Antichrist.
**5. Genre and Style:** A corrective yet comforting Epistle utilizing strong, apocalyptic language concerning fiery judgment.
**6. Language and Translation:** Uses "Grace and Peace" (connecting Greek *Charis* with Hebrew *Eirene*) and describes their endurance as "manifest evidence" of God's judgment.
**7. Theological Themes:** God's righteous judgment, the glory of Christ, the Second Coming, and endurance in suffering.
**8. Immediate Purpose:** To correct escapist attitudes (idleness), strengthen resolve, and clarify the timeline of the end times.

### 1 Timothy
**1. Historical Setting:** Written ~62–63 AD from Macedonia between Paul's Roman imprisonments, roughly 10 years after 1 & 2 Thessalonians.
**2. Cultural Background:** Timothy was stationed in Ephesus, a commercial center where the church struggled with structural organization and the infiltration of "strange doctrines".
**3. Author and Audience:** The Apostle Paul wrote to his mentee Timothy, the young pastor/overseer of the Ephesian church.
**4. Literary Context:** The first of the Pastoral Epistles; a personal letter of leadership instruction rather than a congregational address.
**5. Genre and Style:** A Pastoral Epistle with an instructional, authoritative, and fatherly tone.
**6. Language and Translation:** Key Greek terms include *heterodidaskaleo* (strange doctrines), *oikonomia* (stewardship), and *suneidesis* (conscience).
**7. Theological Themes:** Adherence to sound doctrine, the goal of instruction (love, pure heart, good conscience), the purpose of the Law, and godly stewardship.
**8. Immediate Purpose:** To guide Timothy in managing the church, combat idle false teachers, and encourage his leadership resolve.

### 2 Timothy
**1. Historical Setting:** Written ~66–67 AD from a cold dungeon in Rome during Paul's final imprisonment under Emperor Nero, marking the last letter he ever wrote.
**2. Cultural Background:** After the 64 AD fire of Rome, Christians faced brutal persecution; Paul experienced the harsh reality of dungeon life and abandonment by associates.
**3. Author and Audience:** Paul, facing imminent execution, to his "beloved son" and successor Timothy.
**4. Literary Context:** The final book of the Pastoral Epistles, contrasting with the earlier 1 Timothy as it is written from the brink of death.
**5. Genre and Style:** A Pastoral Epistle acting as a solemn, urgent "Last Will and Testament," employing metaphors of a soldier, athlete, and farmer.
**6. Language and Translation:** Tells Timothy to "stir up" (re-kindle) his gift, and contrasts fear with *dunamis* (power), *agape* (sacrificial love), and *sophronismos* (self-control).
**7. Theological Themes:** Boldness amidst persecution, expected suffering with Christ, God's eternal grace, and the empowering Holy Spirit.
**8. Immediate Purpose:** To pass the spiritual torch, request Timothy to bring his cloak and books, and encourage him to persevere.

### Titus
**1. Historical Setting:** Written ~64 AD from Macedonia or Ephesus; Titus was ministering on the difficult island of Crete between Paul's imprisonments.
**2. Cultural Background:** Crete was notoriously difficult, characterized by its own prophets as a society of "liars, evil beasts, lazy gluttons" with significant moral corruption.
**3. Author and Audience:** The Apostle Paul to Titus, a Greek/Gentile believer who served as a gritty troubleshooter for difficult churches.
**4. Literary Context:** A brief, three-chapter Pastoral Epistle focusing sequentially on leadership, sound doctrine, and Christian conduct.
**5. Genre and Style:** A direct, practical Pastoral Epistle focusing heavily on giving a "to-do list" and emphasizing "good works".
**6. Language and Translation:** Uses *epidiorthoo*, a medical/architectural term meaning to "set in order" or straighten, and bridges cultures with the phrase "common faith".
**7. Theological Themes:** Godly leadership qualifications, the hope of eternal life, sound doctrine vs. fables, and the training nature of grace.
**8. Immediate Purpose:** To organize the chaotic church by appointing elders, silencing insubordinate false teachers, and establishing a lasting foundation.

### Philemon
**1. Historical Setting:** Written ~60–62 AD during Paul's first Roman house arrest; linked directly to the city of Colossae.
**2. Cultural Background:** Slavery was an enormous institution (60 million slaves in the empire), and runaway slaves faced severe, even lethal, legal punishments.
**3. Author and Audience:** Paul wrote to Philemon (a wealthy Christian host), Apphia, and Archippus, serving as an intermediary for Onesimus, a runaway slave.
**4. Literary Context:** A Prison Epistle designed to be read alongside Colossians, as Onesimus is mentioned in both.
**5. Genre and Style:** A brief (25 verses), highly personal, tactful, and persuasive letter appealing to love rather than apostolic command.
**6. Language and Translation:** Plays on the name *Onesimus* (meaning "profitable") and uses *koinonos* to emphasize faith partnership.
**7. Theological Themes:** Forgiveness, the Kinsman Redeemer (imputation of debt), and the Gospel breaking down social barriers to create equality.
**8. Immediate Purpose:** To reconcile Onesimus to his master, offer financial restitution, and transform their relationship from master/slave to brothers.

### Hebrews
**1. Historical Setting:** Written before 70 AD under the Roman Empire, evident because references to animal sacrifices imply the Jewish Temple had not yet been destroyed.
**2. Cultural Background:** Rooted deeply in the Old Testament sacrificial system; Jewish converts faced being disowned and pressured to return to traditional Judaism.
**3. Author and Audience:** The author is unknown (speculated to be Paul, Apollos, or Barnabas), writing to Hebrew Christians (Jewish believers).
**4. Literary Context:** A sustained, structural argument comparing Jesus to major pillars of the Jewish faith (prophets, angels, Moses, priesthood).
**5. Genre and Style:** An Epistle functioning much like a persuasive, comparative sermon utilizing a "Better Than" framework.
**6. Language and Translation:** Uses the language of the Septuagint (Greek Old Testament) to heavily quote Hebrew Scriptures.
**7. Theological Themes:** The absolute supremacy and deity of Christ, perseverance against spiritual laziness, and the reality of the New Covenant.
**8. Immediate Purpose:** To prevent apostasy back to Judaism, encourage believers enduring persecution, and correct legalistic or lazy faith.

### James
**1. Historical Setting:** Written ~45–48 AD, making it the very first book of the New Testament; addressed to believers scattered by early Roman persecution.
**2. Cultural Background:** Deeply Jewish roots (still meeting in "synagogues"); believers faced various trials, poverty, and a culture that highly prized wisdom.
**3. Author and Audience:** Written by James (Jesus' half-brother and head of the Jerusalem church) to the Jewish Christians of the Diaspora.
**4. Literary Context:** Follows Hebrews, moving rapidly from one practical topic to another.
**5. Genre and Style:** An Epistle serving as the blunt, direct "Proverbs of the New Testament," full of nature metaphors and behavioral wisdom.
**6. Language and Translation:** Uses financial terms for "count" (evaluate) and *poikilos* (variegated/multi-colored) to describe diverse trials matched by diverse grace.
**7. Theological Themes:** Faith and works (faith without works is dead), seeking heavenly wisdom, perseverance producing maturity, and the generous character of God.
**8. Immediate Purpose:** To encourage believers in suffering, correct lazy/passive faith, and provide a practical manual for living in a hostile world.

### 1 Peter
**1. Historical Setting:** Written ~64 AD from Rome ("Babylon") just as Emperor Nero launched horrific state-sponsored persecutions against believers in modern-day Turkey.
**2. Cultural Background:** Believers lived as marginalized "pilgrims" facing a literal "fiery trial" (Christians used as human torches).
**3. Author and Audience:** The Apostle Peter to the "pilgrims of the Dispersion" (Gentile and Jewish Christians).
**4. Literary Context:** A five-chapter book leading directly into 2 Peter.
**5. Genre and Style:** An Epistle called the "Job of the New Testament" because of its deeply encouraging, hopeful, yet serious focus on suffering.
**6. Language and Translation:** Uses *Parepidemos* (living alongside but not a citizen), and emphasizes "abundant mercy" and "incorruptible" inheritance.
**7. Theological Themes:** The connection between suffering and eternal glory, a living hope through the resurrection, divine election, and a secure heavenly inheritance.
**8. Immediate Purpose:** To strengthen resolve in the face of the Neronian persecution, reframe trials as refining fires, and remind believers of their heavenly home.

### 2 Peter
**1. Historical Setting:** Written ~65–66 AD from Rome shortly before Peter's execution (crucified upside down).
**2. Cultural Background:** The culture was threatened not by external persecution, but by internal false teachers promoting destructive heresies and worldly "pollutions".
**3. Author and Audience:** The Apostle Simon Peter writing to the same scattered believers who had obtained "like precious faith".
**4. Literary Context:** The final follow-up letter from Peter, moving from cultivating character to condemning false teachers and affirming the Second Coming.
**5. Genre and Style:** An Epistle serving as a "Last Will and Testament"; urgent, confrontational, and corrective.
**6. Language and Translation:** Emphasizes "precious" faith, *epignosis* (precise knowledge), and uses a double negative translating to "you shall never, ever fall".
**7. Theological Themes:** Proactive spiritual growth, the reliability of Holy Spirit-inspired Scripture, God's judgment and hope, and the certainty of the Second Coming.
**8. Immediate Purpose:** To warn against deceptive "wolves," establish doctrinal stability, and exhort Christians to diligence.

### 1 John
**1. Historical Setting:** Written ~85–90 AD; John is the last surviving apostle at around 80 years old, addressing internal doctrinal threats.
**2. Cultural Background:** The church was battling early Gnosticism, which taught that matter is evil and spirit is good, leading to extremes in asceticism or moral license and struggles with the Incarnation.
**3. Author and Audience:** The Apostle John writes as a spiritual grandfather to late first-century believers ("little children").
**4. Literary Context:** While his Gospel was written to produce belief, this epistle was written so believers might *know* they have eternal life.
**5. Genre and Style:** A General Epistle structured like a pastoral sermon relying heavily on stark contrasts (Light vs. Darkness, Truth vs. Error).
**6. Language and Translation:** Key words include *Koinonia* (fellowship), propitiation (satisfactory sacrifice), and "manifested" to describe eternal life becoming tangible.
**7. Theological Themes:** The reality of the Incarnation, walking in the light, the character of God (Light/Love), and the assurance of salvation.
**8. Immediate Purpose:** To combat Gnostic heresy, promote fullness of joy, define true fellowship, and remind believers of forgiveness through confession.

### 2 John
**1. Historical Setting:** Written ~90 AD from Ephesus as an established church faced the threat of traveling false teachers.
**2. Cultural Background:** Deceivers were taking advantage of the cultural norm of hospitality—providing food and lodging to traveling teachers—to infiltrate homes and spread heresy.
**3. Author and Audience:** The Apostle John ("The Elder") to the "Elect Lady and her children," a term of great respect.
**4. Literary Context:** The shortest book in the Bible (13 verses), applying the themes of 1 John to a specific situation.
**5. Genre and Style:** A Personal/Pastoral Epistle using a balanced style that hinges on the relationship between Truth and Love.
**6. Language and Translation:** "Truth" is used five times and "Love" four times in the opening verses; uses "Antichrist" to describe deniers of the Incarnation.
**7. Theological Themes:** The Incarnation, walking in truth, the boundaries of biblical love, and abiding in the doctrine of Christ.
**8. Immediate Purpose:** To warn the "Elect Lady" not to welcome or support traveling false teachers, cutting off their financial fuel and protecting the home.

### 3 John
**1. Historical Setting:** Written ~90 AD from Ephesus as the second-generation church dealt with complexities of institutional leadership and internal power struggles.
**2. Cultural Background:** The early church relied on traveling missionaries, and providing them hospitality was viewed as "working together for the truth"; social reputation was highly valued.
**3. Author and Audience:** The Apostle John ("The Elder") to the "beloved Gaius," while also addressing Diotrephes and Demetrius.
**4. Literary Context:** The final epistle of John (14 verses long), acting as a counterweight to 2 John by warning against being *too narrow* with hospitality.
**5. Genre and Style:** A Personal Letter that is intimate, pastoral, and confrontational regarding church politics.
**6. Language and Translation:** Uses the unique term *philoproteuo* ("loving to be first") for Diotrephes, and language of physical "health" and a "good journey" for prosperity.
**7. Theological Themes:** Truth and love in practical action, Christian hospitality, spiritual vs. physical prosperity, and apostolic authority.
**8. Immediate Purpose:** To commend Gaius for his hospitality, rebuke Diotrephes for his pride and division, and establish a standard for doing good.

### Jude
**1. Historical Setting:** Written ~68–70 AD, before the Temple's destruction; a period of "doctrinal drift" in the second generation of the early church.
**2. Cultural Background:** A culture of religious relativism where subversive men had infiltrated the church to turn grace into an excuse for lewdness and immorality.
**3. Author and Audience:** Written by Jude (half-brother of Jesus) to the general body of called and sanctified believers facing internal threats.
**4. Literary Context:** The penultimate book of the Bible, sharing a strong literary connection with 2 Peter 2 (confirming false teachers *have* arrived).
**5. Genre and Style:** A General Epistle serving as an urgent, punchy, and confrontational "call to arms".
**6. Language and Translation:** Uses *tereo* (preserved/kept) five times, and *epagonizomai* (to agonize/contend earnestly) denoting intense military struggle.
**7. Theological Themes:** Contending for the faith, the danger of apostasy, God as the ultimate "Keeper," and the completed sufficiency of the faith.
**8. Immediate Purpose:** To sound an alarm against godless infiltrators, exhort the church to fight for truth, and provide spiritual discernment.

### Revelation
**1. Historical Setting:** Written ~95 AD by John while exiled to the harsh prison island of Patmos by Roman Emperor Domitian.
**2. Cultural Background:** Intense state-sponsored persecution against Christians; the letter is addressed to seven major cultural/religious hubs in the province of Asia.
**3. Author and Audience:** The Apostle John, a very old eyewitness to Jesus, to the seven churches in Asia (and the broader body of Christ).
**4. Literary Context:** The final book of the Bible, progressing from a vision of the glorified Christ to messages for churches, to visions of the future.
**5. Genre and Style:** Apocalyptic Literature, Prophecy, and Epistle; highly symbolic and visionary, featuring repeated divine instructions to "write these things down".
**6. Language and Translation:** *Apocalypsis* means an "unveiling" or "revealing"; specifically, it is the revelation *of* Jesus Christ, not *of* John.
**7. Theological Themes:** The absolute sovereignty of Jesus (Alpha and Omega), His glorious return, God's faithfulness in suffering, and the ultimate victory of the Kingdom of God.
**8. Immediate Purpose:** To encourage persecuted believers with their eternal reward, exhort readiness for Christ's return, and record a transcript of the divine visions.
